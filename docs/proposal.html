CoolBench

A global, open benchmark for urban heat adaptation planning.

Contribution Benchmark plus baselines Data 100% open, no credentials Phases 0 and 1 passed, 9 cities

The claim

WRI's Cool Cities Lab solved the forward model: given an intervention, how much cooler? Nobody has solved the inverse problem: given a budget, what should you build? CoolBench is a reproducible, physics-grounded environment in which an agent allocates a finite adaptation budget across a real city to minimise heat-attributable exposure under equity and robustness constraints. Built entirely from open global data requiring no account, key or quota, with baselines spanning greedy, MILP, evolutionary, quality-diversity and deep RL, and zero-shot transfer to held-out cities.

Greedy targets correctly, planting where baseline Tmrt averages 64.8°C against an outdoor mean of 55.8°C. Local cooling where planted is 17.84°C mean and 27.19°C maximum.

The finding that shapes the benchmark

Cooling spilled over to unplanted outdoor pixels by 1.52 °C.

That is spatial interaction. A tree shades its neighbours, so the marginal value of any placement depends on every other placement. Greedy assumes independence and structurally cannot see it. The gap between greedy and an interaction-aware search is the headroom this benchmark exists to measure, and it is now quantified rather than asserted. This is the empirical reason evolutionary search, quality-diversity and RL belong in the paper at all.

The physics behaves

At night Tmrt collapses to air temperature with almost no spatial spread. By 15:00 the median reaches 73.0°C with a 30°C gap between deep shade at 43.9°C and full sun at 79.7°C. That gap is the entire premise of the project, now measured from open data rather than assumed.

A search method that knows this can spend its budget better.

What the comparison says

Lagos returns 38% more benefit per dollar than Ahmedabad, despite cooling each planted pixel less. It starts from zero canopy, so every tree lands on genuinely unshaded ground, and its exposed population is more than twice as dense, so each degree of cooling reaches more people.

That points the opposite way to where adaptation money currently goes. The treeless, dense, tropical cities that make up 9% of the literature appear to have the highest return on adaptation spend. Spillover also reproduces across both cities, so spatial interaction is a stable property rather than an Ahmedabad artefact.

The cities that need this most are the least studied, the least funded and the least evaluated. A heat event that used to occur once in fifty years now occurs once a decade.

04

The gap, stated precisely

WRI launched Cool Cities Lab in March 2026: SOLWEIG plus UTCI at 1 m, validated at 0.39°C mean absolute error against LiDAR-derived models, across 25 cities on five continents. It is good science and we adopt the method and cite it. It is also, by its authors' own description, a scenario viewer.

What existsWhat it doesWhy it isn't this
WRI Cool Cities LabForward model. Evaluates predefined scenarios (technical, achievable, programme-level)By WRI's own papers, it does not address optimisation, budget constraints, cost, equity or multi-objective tradeoffs. It answers "how much cooler", never "what should I build".
GUHVI, 2025Global open urban heat vulnerability indexDiagnosis, not decision. Tells you where it hurts, not what to build. We consume it as input.
CityLearn, Building2BuildingGymnasium environments for building energy, HVAC, demand responseIndoor energy management. Nothing outdoors, nothing spatial.
weather2alertGymnasium environment for optimising heat alert issuanceWarning policy, not capital allocation. The closest sibling, and proof the community wants this.
Single-city heat optimisers, 2025 to 2026Deep-learning surrogates plus differential evolution for green and cool roofsOne city, local data, one "optimal" plan, no transfer, no shared protocol, not reproducible elsewhere.

Nobody has built the inverse problem, and nobody has built a shared protocol for comparing methods on it. That absence is the paper.

05

Architecture: one rule

The rule

Credentials at build time, never at use time.

A benchmark that requires three accounts to run is not a benchmark. WRI's cities-cif needs Google Earth Engine access, a Copernicus CDS API key, and an AWS profile named cities-data-dev whose credentials live in WRI's internal secrets. Their OpenUrban land cover is served only as an Earth Engine asset. That is fine for a hosted platform and fatal for a reproducible benchmark.

So the pipeline splits. Build time, done once by us, needs network but no accounts: pull layers, run the physics, design the intervention sampling, fit the surrogate. Use time, for anyone, forever: download a frozen versioned bundle and run offline. This is what separates a benchmark from a pipeline.

Where we can be better, not just faster

WRI uses UT-GLOBUS for building heights, which reports RMSE 9.1 m against LiDAR in its own paper. Three storeys of error in the input that drives shadow geometry, which is why WRI cautions that their 1 m output has spatial inaccuracies "well above 1 m" and advises against site-specific use. We measured the alternatives on the same Ahmedabad area:

SourceBuildings foundCarrying a heightQuery time
Overture 2026-08-19.014,2131 (0.0%)298 s
GlobalBuildingAtlas LoD127,35227,352 (100%)6.0 s

GlobalBuildingAtlas wins on every axis: 1.9 times more buildings, complete heights against Overture's effectively zero, and 50 times faster because it is tiled by 5 degrees so one file covers a city instead of a global scan. Native 3 m resolution, above 97% global height completeness, published in ESSD 2025.

One building in 14,213 carrying a height is a finding, not a configuration choice. It is a measurable North/South data asymmetry that directly caps what heat modelling can claim in exactly the cities that need it most. It belongs in the paper.

07

Four tracks

Track A

Single period

Fixed budget, one shot. MILP and greedy should be strong. If RL loses, that is a finding, and an honest benchmark reports it.

Track B

Multi-period sequencing

Twenty years with tree growth, discounting and budget cycles. RL earns its keep: the state is non-stationary and actions have long delayed payoff.

Track C

Zero-shot transfer

Train on data-rich cities, deploy unseen. Targets a documented failure mode, 46% degradation on a Los Angeles to Beijing transfer, and the 9% equity gap directly.

Track D

Portfolio

Produce a set of behaviourally distinct plans, scored on quality-diversity coverage rather than a single optimum. Planners choose among options under political constraint. Almost no prior work applies quality-diversity to spatial intervention planning, so this is where the sharpest novelty sits.

The measured 1.52°C spillover is what gives Tracks B and D their teeth. Interaction is real and quantified, so a method that models it has something concrete to win.

09

What we will not claim

Being explicit here is what separates this from an overclaiming paper. Phase 0 already turned one of these from a caveat into a caught defect.

Stated limits

  • SOLWEIG models outdoor thermal comfort, not indoor air temperature. Most heat mortality, especially among the over-65s who are roughly 90% of deaths, is mediated by indoor exposure. We report outdoor exposure and do not claim to predict deaths averted. Phase 0 found this as a real bug: Tmrt over a building footprint is not a temperature anyone experiences, yet the dasymetric population step places everyone inside buildings, so the first scoring pass weighted the physics by exactly the mask where it does not apply. Fixed by redistributing residents onto outdoor pixels within walking reach.
  • Exposure-response functions do not exist for most target cities. The MCC network covers 604 locations in 39 countries, heavily skewed to the Global North. We use meta-regression transfer and report where the epidemiology is extrapolated, as a first-class uncertainty.
  • Equity is currently proxied by population density, which is weak. Phase 0 measured a negative equity gap, meaning the densest areas are cooler at pedestrian level by day. That is probably physically real, since dense low-rise fabric self-shades its own streets, but interpreting it needs a proper deprivation surface.
  • A benchmark is not a deployment. We are not telling a city what to build. We are giving the research community a shared, honest place to compare methods.

12

Umbra, the second artifact

Named for the fully shaded core of a shadow, which is literally what SOLWEIG computes: shadow geometry driving mean radiant temperature.

En-ROADS works as a negotiation instrument because it answers in 60 milliseconds. It is global, system-dynamics, and about mitigation, so there is no overlap with this work. The two nest rather than compete: En-ROADS gives the planet's temperature trajectory, Umbra gives where to plant trees on a given block given that trajectory.

The surrogate is the only thing standing between us and that instrument. Once city-scale heat response evaluates in milliseconds rather than minutes, a planner can move a budget slider and watch the exposure map and the health burden update live. That aims directly at the governance failures above: 88% of cities have no heat budget, fewer than 5% ever evaluate an intervention, and the diagnosis is siloed decision-making.

Same core, two audiences. CoolBench is the rigorous artifact for researchers. Umbra is the instrument for the people who actually hold the budget.
